A family ritual · draft one

Second Sunday

The family gets together on the second Sunday of the month. One hour. Whoever wins picks what we do next time.

Then

    The date never needs deciding, which is the point. Nobody proposes Second Sunday. It just is the second Sunday.

    The whole thing

    Three rules, and they are not negotiable

    1

    Second Sunday of every month, same time.

    Pick the time once, as a family, and then never discuss it again. Rescheduling is how this dies — the moment the date is up for negotiation, someone has to campaign for it every month, and that someone burns out.

    2

    One hour. Hard stop.

    End it while people still want more. An hour that ends clean gets people back next month; ninety minutes that fizzle out does not.

    3

    Whoever wins picks and runs the next one.

    This is the load-bearing rule. It makes a new organizer every single month, it means nobody has to keep volunteering, and it means the whole thing survives the person who started it losing interest.

    Run of show

    The hour

    :00

    Doors open, question already on screen

    The first ten minutes are where these things die. Have one question up before anyone arrives — "what's the worst haircut you ever had" beats "so, how's everyone doing." People join mid-answer and that's fine.

    :10

    The activity

    Spelling bee, scavenger hunt, trivia, talent show. Whatever the host picked. Nobody else prepared anything and nobody else should have to.

    :45

    Scores, standings, handoff

    Read the night's scores. Update the Cup. Name who won, and therefore who is running next month — out loud, so they can't quietly not do it.

    :55

    Out

    Goodbyes, and go. If you're mid-round at :55, stop mid-round.

    The scoring

    The Cup

    A running total across the calendar year, reset each January. It exists so that month nine is more interesting than month one, and so there is a reason to come back that isn't guilt.

    What you didPoints
    Showed up3
    Played2
    Won the night4
    Ran the night5

    Show up to all twelve and play: 60 points. Win six nights outright but miss the other six: 54.

    You cannot win the Cup by being good at spelling. You can only win it by being there.

    Miss a month and you miss the points, not the Cup — there are no make-ups and nothing gets moved. Running the night is worth more than winning it, because running it is the harder thing and the thing the family actually needs someone to do.

    Copy this

    What you send

    Keep it flat and slightly bored. Earnestness is what makes people wince — the second an invitation says "let's reconnect as a family," half the recipients decide they have a thing that day.

    The first one · send two weeks out

    Starting a thing: Second Sunday. Second Sunday of every month, 7pm Eastern, one hour, same link every time. This month I'm running a spelling bee. You don't need to prepare anything or install anything — just click the link. Whoever wins picks what we do next month, so if you hate spelling bees, win. [link]

    Every month after · send two days out

    Second Sunday is [date], 7pm Eastern. [Winner] won last month so they're running it — apparently we're doing [thing]. Same link: [link] Current standings: [top three]

    Decide now, not live

    What goes wrong

    Every one of these will happen. Deciding the answer in advance means the host isn't improvising social policy in front of twelve relatives.

    Only three people show up
    Run it anyway, with three. Cancelling once teaches everyone that it's cancellable, and then it is.
    Half the family can't make that date
    It still happens on that date. A ritual that moves for whoever complains loudest isn't a ritual, it's a recurring negotiation.
    The tech falls apart
    Every host keeps one no-software fallback in their pocket. Two truths and a lie, going around the call. Costs nothing, needs nothing, fills twenty minutes.
    It's :55 and you're mid-round
    Stop. Void the round or carry it — nobody minds, and everyone remembers the night that went ninety minutes.
    One person dominates
    That's the activity's fault, not the host's. Only pick things with structured turns where nobody gets eliminated.
    Someone barely speaks the whole hour
    Give them a job. Scorekeeper, timekeeper, judge. A role is much easier to accept than a conversation is to start.
    The kids are bored
    Also a job. A nine-year-old with a stopwatch and the authority to cut people off is the most engaged person on the call.
    The winner doesn't want to host
    They pick the activity and someone else drives. Picking is the part that matters — it's what makes next month theirs.
    The actual experiment

    The first three months

    Month one

    You run it. Spelling bee, because it's already built. This month proves nothing except that people will turn up once, which they will, because you asked them to.

    Month two

    The winner runs it. This is the whole test. Not whether anyone enjoyed month one — everyone enjoys month one. The question is whether the handoff holds when the person running it isn't the person who cares most.

    If you end up running month two yourself, the mechanic failed, and that's the single most useful thing you could have learned this quarter.

    Month three

    It happens without you scheduling it. If someone else sends the reminder, you no longer have an activity. You have a ritual, and rituals are the thing that can travel to families you've never met.

    After that, the only number worth counting: how many families you're not related to run a second one.

    Second Sunday, draft one. The activities are interchangeable and always will be — the date, the hour, and the handoff are the parts that have to stay fixed.
